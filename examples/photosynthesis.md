# Photosynthesis, one step at a time

Every meal you have ever eaten was, at some point, sunlight. Plants, algae and
some bacteria run a process that takes three cheap ingredients — light, water and
carbon dioxide — and builds sugar out of them. Photosynthesis is how energy gets
into the living world in the first place, and the details are more interesting
than the summary equation suggests.

## The one-line version

Chemists write it like this:

    6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂

Six molecules of carbon dioxide and six of water, powered by light, become one
molecule of glucose and six of oxygen. Read the arrow as "many steps, over two
distinct stages, in two different places inside the leaf". The oxygen on the right
is not the point of the reaction. It is a waste product — one that happens to have
rebuilt the atmosphere and made animals possible.

## Where it happens

Follow the ingredients into a leaf.

- **Light** lands on the upper surface and passes into cells packed with
  **chloroplasts**, the green organelles where the whole process runs.
- **Carbon dioxide** enters through **stomata**, adjustable pores on the
  underside of the leaf.
- **Water** arrives from the roots through the veins.

Inside a chloroplast are stacks of flattened membrane discs called **thylakoids**,
sitting in a fluid called the **stroma**. That division matters: the first stage
of photosynthesis happens in the thylakoid membranes, the second in the stroma.

## Stage one: capturing the light

The thylakoid membranes are studded with **chlorophyll**, a pigment that absorbs
red and blue light strongly and reflects green — which is the only reason plants
look green to us.

When a chlorophyll molecule absorbs a photon, one of its electrons jumps to a
higher energy level. That excited electron is passed down a chain of proteins like
a bucket in a bucket brigade, and at each handover a little energy is released and
used to pump protons across the membrane. The proton gradient that builds up drives
a turbine-like enzyme, **ATP synthase**, which manufactures ATP: the cell's
standard unit of usable energy.

The chlorophyll is now missing an electron, and this is where water comes in. The
plant **splits water** to replace it, taking the electrons it needs and discarding
what is left: oxygen gas, which diffuses out through the stomata and into the air
you are breathing right now.

The stage ends with two products, both energy carriers rather than food: **ATP**
and **NADPH**.

## Stage two: building the sugar

The second stage is called the **Calvin cycle**, and it needs no light directly.
It runs in the stroma, spending the ATP and NADPH the first stage produced.

The key player is an enzyme usually shortened to **RuBisCO** — plausibly the most
abundant protein on Earth. It grabs a molecule of carbon dioxide from the air and
attaches it to a five-carbon sugar. The resulting molecule is unstable, splits,
and is then rebuilt, step by step, using the energy carriers from stage one. Most
of the product is recycled to keep the cycle turning; the surplus is drawn off as
sugar.

Fix six carbon dioxide molecules and you can assemble one glucose. Some of that
glucose is burned immediately for energy, some becomes cellulose for structure,
and some is stored as starch for the night — because stage two can run in the dark
only until the ATP runs out.

## Two stages, one dependency

The cleanest way to hold the whole process in your head is as a supply chain.

- The **light reactions** convert light into chemical energy, and produce oxygen
  as a by-product.
- The **Calvin cycle** spends that chemical energy to convert carbon dioxide into
  sugar.

Neither half works alone. The light reactions make energy carriers nothing else
would use; the Calvin cycle needs an energy supply it cannot generate. The
efficiency of the whole thing is only a few percent of the sunlight that falls on
a leaf — and that few percent supports very nearly every ecosystem on the planet.
